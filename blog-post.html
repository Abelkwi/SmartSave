"I Used to Sell at Whatever Price They Offered. Now I Know What My Harvest Is Worth."

Marie Uwamahoro has farmed in Musanze for eight years. For most of them, she sold tomatoes at the farm gate to middlemen who set the price. This is the story of how real-time market data changed her income — and her confidence.

On a Wednesday morning in early June, Marie Uwamahoro is walking between rows of tomatoes on her 1.2-hectare farm outside Musanze. The soil here, in the shadow of the Virunga volcanoes, is deep and dark and rich with nutrients that have been building for centuries. She has been farming this land for eight years. For most of those years, she would wait at the edge of the road in the mornings for the traders to pass.

The traders set the price. Sometimes it was fair. Mostly, it was not. "They knew I could not take the tomatoes to Kigali myself," she says, picking a ripe one and turning it in her hand. "They knew I had to sell. So the price was whatever they wanted to give me."

"They knew I could not take the tomatoes to Kigali myself. They knew I had to sell. So the price was whatever they wanted to give me."

— Marie Uwamahoro, Musanze District

This is the reality for the majority of smallholder farmers across Rwanda. Not a lack of productivity — Marie's yields are strong. Not a lack of quality — her tomatoes are consistently firm, well-coloured, and large. The gap between what food is worth and what farmers receive for it is not a problem of agriculture. It is a problem of information and connection.

Discovering What Her Tomatoes Were Actually Worth

In early 2025, a field officer from Akagera Farmers Cooperative visited Marie's sector to talk about a new platform called SMARTSAVE. The pitch was simple: register, list your produce, and buyers across Rwanda will be able to see what you have and contact you directly.

Marie was sceptical. "I had heard about programmes like this before," she says. "They come, they explain something, and then nothing changes." But she registered anyway, entered her available stock of tomatoes, and waited.

The first thing she noticed was not a buyer request. It was the market price display on her dashboard — showing what tomatoes were currently selling for at Kigali Central Market. That day, the platform showed 480 RWF per kilogram. The trader who had passed that morning had offered her 320 RWF.

"That was when I understood," she says. "The difference was not because my tomatoes were bad. It was because I had no way to know what they were worth — and they did."

Her First Direct Order

Three days after registering, Marie received a message through SMARTSAVE from the procurement manager at a hotel in Kigali. They needed 200 kilograms of Roma tomatoes, delivered within 48 hours, at 450 RWF per kilogram.

She had never sold directly to a hotel before. The order felt enormous. She called the cooperative coordinator, Emmanuel Kalisa, who helped her confirm the order, arrange transport through the cooperative's truck network, and process the MoMo payment. Within 48 hours, she had delivered her first institutional order.

The hotel placed a second order the following week. Then a school. Then a restaurant. Over the course of her first full season on SMARTSAVE, Marie completed 12 orders through the platform — mostly direct institutional buyers she had never had access to before. Her income for that season was 34% higher than the year before.

Practical Tip from Marie's Experience
When listing produce on SMARTSAVE, Marie recommends being specific about harvest date, available quantity, and packaging options. "Buyers want to know exactly what they are getting before they place an order. The more precise you are, the faster they respond."

Beyond Income — What Changed Most

Marie pauses when asked what changed most about her farming after joining SMARTSAVE. She does not say income, though she mentions it. She says planning.

"Before, I planted what I could and hoped someone would buy it. Now I can see what buyers are looking for. I can see which vegetables are in demand. I planted more climbing beans this season because I could see the price was strong and a school in Kigali had asked for regular supply."

This shift — from reactive selling to proactive planning — is one of the most significant but least-discussed benefits of market connectivity for smallholder farmers. When farmers know what their buyers need in advance, they can align their planting decisions to real demand rather than guessing.

The Storage Gap She Did Not Know She Had

One of the less expected benefits came from SMARTSAVE's storage guidance feature. During her second month on the platform, Marie received a notification with crop-specific post-harvest tips for tomatoes — including temperature recommendations, stacking advice, and the importance of harvesting slightly before full ripeness if the tomatoes would travel more than two hours.

"I did not know that," she says. "I was picking them when they were fully ripe. By the time they reached Kigali, some were already soft. I was losing maybe 8% of every delivery to spoilage."

She adjusted her picking schedule. The losses stopped. The hotel procurement manager commented that the quality had noticeably improved. Her price per kilogram held steady rather than being renegotiated downward, which is common when buyers notice quality inconsistency.

About Post-Harvest Tomato Loss

Research from Rwanda Agriculture Board estimates that 15–30% of Rwanda's tomato harvest is lost post-harvest annually — primarily due to premature softening, improper stacking, and delayed market access. Most of these losses are preventable with low-cost interventions and better market timing.

Looking Ahead

This season, Marie has 1.2 hectares planted with tomatoes, green bell peppers, and climbing beans. She has standing orders with two hotels and a school that repeat monthly. She is currently in discussion with a WFP procurement officer about supplying vegetables for a school feeding programme in Gasabo District.

"I would have said two years ago that was impossible," she says, smiling. "A WFP order? From my small farm? But now I am part of a cooperative, and together we can do that."

Her daughter, who studies at the University of Rwanda, has started helping her manage the SMARTSAVE dashboard in the evenings — tracking order history, reviewing prices, and looking at the analytics that show which products are performing best. Marie sees this as exactly what the platform is supposed to do: connect generations, connect skills, connect the knowledge her daughter brings home from university with the knowledge Marie has built in the soil over eight years.

"Food does not just feed people," she says, looking back at the rows of tomatoes behind her. "If it is managed well, it builds everything. It builds income. It builds education. It builds a future." She pauses. "That is what SMARTSAVE means to me."
